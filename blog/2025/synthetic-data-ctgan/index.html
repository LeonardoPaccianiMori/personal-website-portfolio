When CTGAN Failed to Preserve the Correlations That Mattered

A practical synthetic-data decision from an Italian real-estate project: preserving geographic and price correlations mattered more than matching marginal distributions.

May 15, 2025 6 min read
machine-learning synthetic-data algorithms

For the Italian real estate project, I needed a public-facing dashboard that preserved useful aggregate structures and correlations without displaying source listings.

This constraint was central to the project because I wanted the dashboard to reflect patterns from the study without displaying or distributing the collected records.

The obvious choice was CTGAN. On paper it looked exactly like what I was looking for:

  • purpose-built for tabular data
  • widely used
  • relatively easy to train
  • good at reproducing distributions

If only each feature’s distribution was relevant to this project, CTGAN would have worked flawlessly.

The Problem Was Not Distribution Shape

My real constraint, however, was not just to generate synthetic data that looks plausible row by row, but actually preserve the relationships between the features that make the later model and dashboard actually meaningful.

With real estate data, this means things like the relationship between:

  • location and price
  • surface area and price
  • neighborhood context and rent
  • property type and geography

CTGAN reproduced the marginal distributions reasonably well. Prices looked like reasonable prices (there were no listings priced at EUR 1,500 or EUR 10 trillion). Surface areas looked like surface areas (there were no 2sqm or 1,000,000sqm listings). Categories appeared in roughly the right proportions.

However, some location-dependent relationships degraded badly, even though simpler relationships like surface area and price were mostly preserved.

That was a dealbreaker, because the downstream model relied on exactly those relationships. If the synthetic data distorted them, the model would learn from a distorted version of the market rather than from the structure I cared about preserving.

The Validation That Changed the Decision

A simple check on the data generated by CTGAN showed that this was not the right route for synthetic data generation with this dataset.

I took three of the most important features identified by the rental income model predictor (the listing’s location, surface, and a binary Milan indicator), and compared their relationships with the listing’s price in the real and synthetic data. The picture was quite grim:

Metric Real Data CTGAN
Location ↔ Price R² 0.62 0.03
Surface ↔ Price R² 0.71 0.69
Milan price premium +45% +2%


CTGAN reproduced the correlation between surface and price, but completely missed the relationship between location and price, as well as the “Milan price premium”. This failure is understandable: while the relationship between surface and price can reasonably be assumed to be roughly linear, or at least monotonic (the larger a property is, the more it will cost on average), the relationship between location (meant as a (latitude, longitude) point) and price is not linear or monotonic, so it is more complicated for a model to learn.

For this reason, I developed a custom KNN-based synthetic data generator, which preserved the specific relationships I needed for this project much more closely than CTGAN did:

Metric Real Data CTGAN Custom KNN
Location ↔ Price R² 0.62 0.03 0.58
Surface ↔ Price R² 0.71 0.69 0.70
Milan price premium +45% +2% +42%


The Simpler Alternative Was Better

I switched to a custom K-Nearest-Neighbors generator built around a simple idea: if the point of synthetic data is to preserve local market structure, then each synthetic listing should be built from nearby real listings in feature space.

Therefore, for each synthetic record I have:

  1. picked an anchor point (i.e., one listing in the real, scraped data)
  2. identified the 5 “closest” listings in the real dataset, where the “distance” was calculated using price, surface, latitude, and longitude
  3. created a new, synthetic listing by blending all numerical features of these 5 nearest neighbors by weighted average (where the weights are inversely proportional to the distance used in the previous point)
  4. chose categorical features for this new synthetic listing by weighted voting
  5. sampled boolean features probabilistically from the same neighborhood

This method preserved the target relationships much more directly, by construction, instead of hoping a generative model would infer them robustly from scratch. It produced new rows rather than copies of source listings. However, because it was built from local neighborhoods in the source data and was not subjected to a formal privacy audit, I now treat it as an analytical transformation—not as a certified anonymization mechanism. The row-level synthetic output is therefore not distributed; only aggregate study results remain public.

Since I wanted to recreate a total number of listings similar to the original dataset (i.e., ~1 million), I used CUDA/TensorFlow to take advantage of GPU acceleration to make the data generation much faster.

The Lesson I Learned

Developing this custom algorithm was a good reminder that cookie-cutter approaches are not always the way to go. We are in a time now where choosing to use one model instead of another is just as simple as changing which function from a Python library we use, without necessarily needing to know all the inner workings of a given model. While this is a very good thing, as it makes our life incredibly easier, it is also a double-edged sword: just blindingly using a model without stopping to think what is going on under the hood will eventually lead to situations where something’s not working as we expected.

Really understanding what each model is doing is essential in order to be able to get out of such situations, for example building a custom algorithm like I did.
