_Fahrenheit 451_ is a great classic, which over the past decade has seen a significant surge in sales alongside 1984 (not difficult to understand why[^1], unfortunately).

What struck me most about the book is the explanation of how society in that story abandoned books and fell into totalitarianism, given by Beatty (the firemen's captain). I initially accepted his account as the novel's warning: people gradually lose their tolerance for controversial or offensive ideas until censorship can be presented as protection from discomfort. Yet this is also the rationalisation of someone who enthusiastically _serves_ the system. This ambiguity makes the justification even more interesting, because repression becomes easier to accept when it sounds reasonable and benevolent.

The warning feels particularly relevant in an environment like today's society, shaped by simplified content and echo chambers. Social platforms make it easy to consume oversimplified versions of complex issues and surround ourselves with views we already share. The danger is not only that challenging ideas might be prohibited, but that we may lose the _patience_ and _willingness_ to encounter them. Discomfort and disagreement are not inherently harmful; they are often necessary for serious thought, even when they occasionally offend us.

Bradbury's chaotic, dreamlike writing style was not to my taste. I prefer prose that feels clearer and more orderly, although his writing never made the story difficult to follow. Despite that reservation, the novel's warning stayed with me, and I would recommend it broadly. It is unfortunately a very good time to read this novel very carefully.
